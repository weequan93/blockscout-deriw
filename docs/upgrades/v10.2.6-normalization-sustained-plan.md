long-running job or change PostgreSQL settings. The first implementation is the
[reusable client and maintenance runbook](v10.2.6-normalization-session.md).

model; full-table capacity and vacuum throughput remain to be measured live.

## What the measurements establish

The first reusable session added 4.79 MiB of index allocation, with unchanged
heap allocation and 11,763 additional estimated dead rows. There was no vacuum;
the total dead-row estimate was 36,840. Its 91 HOT updates represent 0.77% of
the 11,764 updates. WAL directory allocation stayed at 368 MiB, with no slots.
This does not demonstrate index/heap space recycling during sustained updates.

The 12 transaction-check measurements total 15.508 seconds, ranging from
1.073 to 1.475 seconds. Host health checks, transport, journaling and preparation
delay are outside those timings. The later filesystem reading shows 888.45 GiB
free and 139.87 MiB net consumption since preparation; it also includes unrelated
writers and post-run time. The two-slot journal uses 3.22 MiB, and all new session
journals together use 4.70 MiB. The aggregate cap remains 256 MiB.

The latest finite run committed 18,347 rows in 19 transactions. Individual
transaction checks took 929–1,462 ms. Successive batch preparations were roughly
20 seconds apart, including release checks, client startup, inspection and
journaling. Those separate costs make the current tool useful for finite pilots;
its end-to-end rate is not a full-table performance forecast.

The measured chunk WAL sum was 122.20 MiB; net filesystem consumption was
177.68 MiB, including unrelated writers and private files. The last two batches
produced more observed WAL than earlier ones. Full-page writes are on and WAL
compression is off, but these measurements do not isolate the reason for that
variation. There are no replication slots and archiving is off in the captured
resource report. `max_wal_size=1024MB` is not a filesystem hard limit.

The catalog estimates 1,776,221,184 rows, with approximately 592 GB of heap and
987 GB of indexes (decimal units). Only 121 of the first 6,797 updates were HOT.
The normal update/delete autovacuum threshold is approximately 355 million dead
tuples at the observed settings; no table-specific override was present. A
sustained update cannot assume that the current vacuum policy will control its
space use. The xmin age in the report is measured in transaction IDs, not time;
it is not proof of a particular long-running session.

## Execution requirements

The opt-in [`lean-v1` execution path](v10.2.6-normalization-session.md#reduce-preparation-overhead)
implements the first overhead reduction: four client requests per fresh update
batch instead of seven. It combines existing read-only preparation and relies
on the unchanged in-transaction prechecks, while keeping pending reconciliation
separate after interruption. No write allowance is reopened. The next live
step still depends on the four-hour vacuum result and reuse review. Larger
batches and parallel writers remain separate experiments after this baseline.

1. Preserve an immutable source/image/database/heap identity and the current
   hold on all automatic migration workers. Never restart the historical
   duplicate-reindex worker whose completion record already exists.
2. Reuse a bounded SQL client for a finite set of transactions to reduce repeated
   startup overhead. Keep application readiness and identity checks before and
   after writes, with a defined maximum gap between observations. Do not start
   the unbounded global worker or its `max(block_number)` search.
3. Keep indexed, bounded selection and exact row/size limits. Record the pending
   target and reconciliation hashes durably before requesting an update. Advance
   a cursor only after committed semantics and runtime checks are verified.
4. Retain the existing atomic statement/time/WAL checks. Lost responses must
   reconcile without another update, and unknown WAL must consume its allowance.
   A finished run must remain finished rather than reset its budget.
5. Separate epoch limits from transaction limits. The new observation profile
   allows at most 250 transactions/250,000 selected rows, 4 GiB charged chunk WAL,
   2 GiB net space/table/retained-WAL growth and a 30-minute new-write window.
   Transactions retain at most 1,000 rows and 128 MiB charged WAL, bounded SQL
   deadlines and the existing 200 GiB free-space floor. This is an observation
   envelope, not an extrapolated full-table capacity budget.
6. Preserve one maintenance resource/filesystem baseline and a 250,000-row phase
   allowance across successor journals, including successors using the legacy
   profile. The observation phase stops for vacuum review at that boundary;
   another preparation cannot reset it. Keep the original v1 limit dictionaries
   and frozen clients valid; v2 profiles are selected only for new journals.

## Vacuum, storage and journals

Measure index/heap growth, dead tuples, vacuum progress, effective table settings,
retained WAL and snapshot age over controlled intervals. Do not infer working
space by multiplying a small pilot's WAL or filesystem observations.

The reusable client replaces the original pilot's `SHARE ROW EXCLUSIVE` table
lock with REPEATABLE READ and bounded row locks. A disposable PostgreSQL 15
check verifies coexistence with ordinary vacuum's table lock and serialization
rollback on a concurrent row update. A later residual pass must account for
inserts committed after each transaction snapshot.

Keep the operator's already accepted manual, unverified recovery policy; do not
introduce another full-database snapshot requirement. Retain all existing pilot
journals. A sustained implementation needs an explicit bounded policy for new
raw snapshots and logs, with durable hashes/checkpoints and reconciliation data
for the pending transaction. Retaining every full row snapshot across the whole
table has not been budgeted. Any new retention behavior must be concrete in its
implementation and run description before use.

The captured autovacuum settings inherit 512 MiB of maintenance memory and a
vacuum cost limit of 200, with 2 ms cost delay. Manual vacuum currently has zero
cost delay. `track_io_timing` is off: relation block counters distinguish shared
buffer hits/reads but do not measure physical device latency. The default vacuum
trigger is about 355 million dead rows, so the next bounded observation will
not automatically establish vacuum performance. Global settings and table
options remain unchanged.

The next vacuum qualification should pause new normalization epochs, confirm
the checkpoint has no pending writes and establish a dedicated ordinary vacuum
client with pinned database/heap identity. Its proposed settings are local to
that connection: 512 MiB maintenance memory, cost limit 200, cost delay 2 ms and
500 ms lock timeout. Use `TRUNCATE OFF` to avoid the truncation lock and
`PARALLEL 0` to bound maintenance concurrency. Index cleanup must be included in
at least one completed qualification cycle; `INDEX_CLEANUP ON` requests it even
with relatively few dead tuples. With roughly 987 GB of indexes, that can be
substantial work and must have its own monitored execution window. This is a
plan implemented by the separate monitored runner. Two live windows reached
index cleanup but neither completed a cycle; the latest evidence is recorded
in the [vacuum review](v10.2.6-vacuum.md).

The vacuum runner must first record a private PID/backend-start/application
identity and fixed statement fingerprint. Observe progress, retained WAL, free
space and application health at most 30 seconds apart; retain phase/counter
observations, initial/final table statistics and the private VERBOSE result.
Pause on service health failure or an unreleased old snapshot. A duration,
storage or I/O limit must cancel only that revalidated maintenance statement;
it must not terminate another session. Interrupted vacuum is useful evidence
but cannot qualify a completed heap/index cycle. Neither statistics-counter
reduction nor filesystem shrinkage alone proves successful space recycling.

Before continuous operation, require a completed ordinary vacuum cycle including
index cleanup, observed dead-row reduction and subsequent update intervals that
demonstrate reusable-space behavior at an acceptable write rate. Measure its
duration and heap/index progress to choose normalization pacing and maintenance
windows. Only then select table-specific autovacuum/analyze thresholds and cost
settings, retaining the original reloptions for exact restoration. A one-million
row vacuum threshold with scale factor zero remains a candidate, not an applied
policy; repeated index cleanup at that threshold may be expensive. PostgreSQL
documents the index-cleanup tradeoff, truncation lock and vacuum progress
interfaces in its [VACUUM reference](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/15/sql-vacuum.html).

Ordinary vacuum generally makes space reusable inside the existing relations;
filesystem free space need not rise. No VACUUM FULL, table rewrite, index removal
or unrelated session termination belongs to this normalization phase. Keep all
legacy pilot/sample/batch/run journals. New sessions continue to retain two raw
slots, compact checkpoint/hash/timing totals and the last 25 receipts, within
64 MiB per directory and 256 MiB in aggregate. The read-only retention inventory
protects all unfinished/pending sessions and the newest two directories; older
finished new sessions are manual archival candidates only. Preserve their
private permissions and verify a successor before archiving its predecessors.

## Completion boundary

A stored-row cursor does not prove missing historical traces were fetched.
Historical RPC and the trace fetcher remain held. Review null block numbers,
concurrent inserts and the upstream migration's exact completion semantics
before changing its completion record/cache. Unique-index creation, not-null
validation, PK conversion and the two deferred custom index migrations each
retain their separate space and compatibility review.

The application can continue on the verified v10 release while this work remains
held. Deployment/configuration handover can be completed independently of a
full-table data rewrite.
